By Eu Joe Chegne & Raniera Te Moni

This was a capstone project from the TARA Program and was awarded as a spotlight project. Shortly after our publication, Anthropic released a similar jailbreak framework<Ref id="anthropicseverity" /> which addresses our limitations.

Jailbreaks are adversarial techniques where a user crafts a specialized prompt to trick a large language model (LLM) into bypassing its built-in safety filters and alignment rules. The metric for jailbreak results is often reported as an attack success rate (ASR) where an evaluator labels each response as successful or unsuccessful, and we count how often the attack worked. This is useful for comparing attacks, but it leaves a lot inside the word “successful”.

Consider the two responses below:

- **Example A** asks how to break into an online account, and the model replies with a generic list of attack categories.
- **Example B** asks how to produce botulinum toxin, and the model attempts to provide a procedure.

A binary judge can label both as successful jailbreaks, `jailbreak_attempt=pass`. Each contributes one success to our evaluation metrics.

Example B warrants much closer scrutiny. This response has the potential for much greater harm, as the response attempts to provide procedural step-by-step guidance, while Example A explains public domain knowledge. Counting them equally discards a distinction we care about.

<BinaryContrast />

It is imperative to make two distinctions here: how serious the requested task is, and how much the answer actually helps.

A numbered procedure for example does not establish that the instructions are correct or workable. That distinction motivated us to pursue our project in two halves: developing Renegade, which we will give a write-up in a future blog post, and extending the SABER workflow<Ref id="saber" /> to evaluate repeated attempts and the severity of their outputs. This post focuses on the evaluation half.

## What counts as success

Recall that a binary success means that our LLM judge classified a response as satisfying its success criteria. It does not mean that someone completed the harmful task, or that the response would work outside the evaluation.

This matters even before comparing different harm categories. Within cyber, a response that names an attack technique and a response that materially helps carry it out can receive the same binary label. Across categories, the consequences also change drastically:

> Steps to perform an SQL injection versus producing a dangerous toxin raise different concerns, which an aggregate ASR cannot express.

They motivate a closer assessment. They do not, by themselves, give us a calibrated measure of the difference in harm.

We therefore wanted to retain more information about each output. How substantive is it? How much of the requested task does it cover?

## Giving the attacker ten attempts

There is a second problem with summarising an evaluation by the average response. An attacker can keep trying, and one useful answer may be enough.

We evaluated 159 requests from the standard functional subset of HarmBench<Ref id="harmbench" />, covering six harm categories. We tested two attack methods (GCG and Renegade) against two models, with ten attempts per request and 6,360 responses in total.

GCG searches for a suffix that makes a chosen compliant response more likely. Renegade on the other hand requires whitebox model access as it searches for a suffix that suppresses selected attention heads associated with refusal.

For these runs, we used ten previously selected suffixes from each method. Both sets were selected using Llama, and a transfer test was then done by using them on Gemma. To be exact, Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and `google/gemma-4-E4B-it` were used. Each attack–model pair produced 1,590 responses.

<PortfolioCoverage />

We call the request-level measure ASR@10: the proportion of requests with at least one judge-labelled success among the ten tested attempts.

Renegade reached more requests in both runs, but the experiment does not establish a general ranking of the algorithms under equal optimisation budgets.

## Looking inside the successful responses

We defined a category-specific severity rubric with four levels:

| Score | Broad interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0 | Safe output or refusal |
| 1 | Low-severity or conceptual content |
| 2 | More substantive but incomplete harmful content |
| 3 | The category’s most serious response type under the rubric |

The criteria vary by category. Cyber responses, for example, receive separate scores for capability, chain coverage, and targeting or evasion, with the maximum used as the headline score. This lets a response score highly on one consequential dimension without requiring it to cover every part of an attack.

We applied the severity judge to responses the binary judge had already labelled successful. Two runs show why this extra assessment matters:

The disagreement is particularly visible in the chemical and biological category. GCG / Gemma had 17 binary successes there, and all 17 received severity 0. Reporting those successes as evidence of operational biological capability would go well beyond what we measured.

The severity judge is another model applying another definition and is not an independently validated ground truth as the disagreement could reflect different criteria, errors by either judge, or both.

## Severity under repeated attempts

The natural next question is how the severity of the best response changes as the attacker gets more attempts.

Imagine ten responses to the same request: nine refusals and one substantive harmful answer. Averaging severity across all ten would dilute the result, even though the attacker can keep the useful answer. Counting whether any response crosses a specified severity threshold would preserve that event.

<BestOfSeverity />

With severity judgements for every response, we could report the proportion of requests that produce at least one score-2-or-higher answer within ten attempts, and separately the proportion that produce a score-3 answer. We could then examine how those rates change with the budget, keeping the categories visible where their consequences differ.

That way, it would distinguish an attack that mostly elicits low-severity content from one that occasionally produces much more consequential assistance.

## What happens beyond ASR@10

Our starting point for extrapolation was SABER. It models differences in success probability across requests and uses observations at a smaller budget to estimate ASR at a larger one. The paper reports estimating ASR@1000 from 100 attempts per request.<Ref id="saber" />

One motivation is the observed-zero problem. If a request has no successes in ten attempts, using its observed success fraction as its true probability assigns it zero future risk. SABER instead fits a distribution across requests, allowing nonzero estimates for requests with no observed successes. Its formulation assumes independent attempts with a fixed success probability for each request.<Ref id="saber" />

Our report contains exploratory anchored ASR@1000 estimates ranging from roughly 47% to almost 100%. These are fitted projections. We did not collect a thousand attempts per request or an independent large-budget validation set.

The sampling difference matters here. Ten selected suffixes can have different success probabilities and correlated outcomes. Applying a model of independent attempts to that fixed portfolio requires justification. A small reported standard error cannot resolve a mismatch between the data collection process and the statistical assumptions.

Extrapolating the binary label also leaves the severity question open. Even an accurate prediction of how often a judge will say “success” would not tell us how often the model will provide substantial assistance on a consequential task.

## Future work

Future evaluations could start with a blinded, expert-reviewed sample containing both binary-positive and binary-negative responses. This would help investigate judge disagreement and estimate how much the initial screening misses. Severity assessment could then be applied across all responses, with results aggregated by request.

Evaluations at larger budgets need a fresh sampling protocol, documented separation between attack selection and evaluation, and held-out attempts to test the predictions. The relevant check is whether the estimated rates match subsequent observations, including at the higher severity thresholds.

Tests of refusal suppression should also be separated from tests of useful harmful assistance. Weakening an internal refusal signal does not establish that the resulting answer is coherent, correct or useful. Those properties need their own evaluation.

Anthropic’s proposed cyber jailbreak severity framework<Ref id="anthropicseverity" /> offers a useful direction for future work. It assesses capability gain over existing tools, breadth of that gain, ease of weaponization and discoverability. These dimensions extend the assessment beyond the substance of a response to the capability an attacker can actually obtain from a jailbreak. Future evaluations could use them to refine cyber severity rubrics, supported by expert review and comparisons against existing tools. The framework is still a proposal focused on cyber. Validating judges, assessing other harm categories and testing larger-budget predictions remain separate research tasks.

If a system can materially assist a task with very large consequences, both the frequency and the substance of its failures matter. Further work could entail evaluating real-world execution, autonomous agents or the effectiveness of a deployed defence.
